# Coach the Stage First, Then the Fault

Lee Tennis Co should choose every drill in the same order: place the student on a ball stage by what they can do, pick the skill area from a ladder that runs from fed balls to scored live points, and fix faults by changing the task (a target, a zone, a catch, a scoring rule) rather than by explaining technique. The stage decides almost everything else. It sets the court length (36 ft red, 60 ft orange, 78 ft green and yellow), the ball and racquet, and the session length: USTA plans run 45 minutes at pre-rally, 60 at red and early orange, and 90 from orange 2 upward ([USTA Net Generation Red plan](https://www.usta.com/content/dam/usta/content-fragments/tennis-service-center/assets/pdfs/Net-Generation-Practice-Plan-Red.pdf)). It also sets how long one activity can hold attention and which loads are off limits, such as kick serves before about 13 ([AMSSM](https://www.sportsmedtoday.com/tennis-ct-73.htm)) or timed sprint tests for under-tens. Scaled equipment matters more than most parents assume. Red-ball rallies averaged **7.36 shots versus 3.83** with a full-size ball ([Fitzpatrick et al.](https://shura.shu.ac.uk/13997/)), and 7- to 9-year-olds succeeded on **58% of shots with scaled equipment versus 45%** with standard gear ([Larson & Guggenheimer](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3761839)). USTA Net Generation, Tennis Canada, the LTA, Tennis Australia and the ITF share one lesson skeleton (warm-up, short skill blocks, a game, a take-home challenge) and largely the same fixes. That means a compact library of about 30 games can serve five-year-olds through seventy-year-olds by changing the ball, the court and how the point starts. The research confirmed about 300 YouTube demonstrations by exact title and channel but did not watch them, so preview each video before assigning it. Three caveats run through all of this: no federation publishes a junior serve count, the age bands for each colour conflict, and the core USTA curricula date from 2017 to 2019.

## Ball stage, not birthday, sets the court, the clock and the load

The ITF Rules of Tennis fix the legal ranges. A red court is **36 to 42 ft long and 14 to 20 ft wide with a 31.5 to 33 in net**, and an orange court is **58 to 60 ft by 20 to 27 ft with a 31.5 to 36 in net** ([ITF 2026 Rules](https://www.itftennis.com/media/7221/2026-rules-of-tennis-english.pdf)). Balls step up in bounce: red rebounds 85 to 105 cm, orange 105 to 120 cm, green 120 to 135 cm and a standard yellow ball 135 to 147 cm (same source). Tennis Australia's coverage of the 10-and-under rule change described red as 75% slower than a yellow ball, orange as 50% slower and green as 25% slower ([Tennis Australia, 2012](https://www.tennis.com.au/news/2012/01/03/itf-makes-changes-to-rules-of-tennis-for-10u-comp)).

Federations agree on the sequence but not on the ages. Orange is 8 to 10 for the ITF, 8 to 9 for the LTA and 9 to 10 on a Tennis Australia partner page. Yellow begins at 10+ for the LTA, about 11 for the USTA and 12+ for Tennis Australia ([LTA Youth stages](https://clubspark.lta.org.uk/LlanelliTSC/EventsV2/LTAYouth); [YMCA Hot Shots](https://leisurecity.ymca.org.au/tennis/hot-shots); [USTA Midwest progression](https://www.usta.com/en/home/play/youth-tennis/programs/midwest/youth-progression.html)). Every federation calls these ages guides, and the LTA places children "based on their ability". **Assessment, not birth year, is therefore the entry point for every student.**

Scaled equipment does more than make tennis easier. Fitzpatrick, Davids and Stone filmed 48 juniors over 1,010 points. Rallies averaged 7.36 ± 6.06 shots at red against 3.83 ± 2.40 with the full ball, and **forehands made up 66.4% of shots versus 45.96%** ([SHURA](https://shura.shu.ac.uk/13997/)). Buszard and colleagues found children hit the most successful forehands with the smallest racquet and the least-compressed ball, and with that setup they more often swung low to high and met the ball in front ([Buszard et al. 2014](https://www.thefa.com/-/media/education-hub/files/buszard-et-al-2014-effect-of-modifying-tennis-equipment-on-early-skill-development)). In other words, a child moved up too early loses about half the shots per rally and a large share of forehand repetitions, which is the opposite of what an ambitious parent is hoping for.

### Session shape: play first, short blocks, little talk

USTA's Net Generation plans use one skeleton at every level: a warm-up, a character theme capped at 3 minutes, athletic and tennis skills, games, and a "play at home" challenge. Sessions last **45 minutes at pre-rally, 60 minutes from Red 3 to Orange 3, and 90 minutes from Orange 2 through Yellow**. The game block grows from about **22 to 27% of the session at pre-rally to 33 to 44% at yellow** ([Net Gen Red](https://www.usta.com/content/dam/usta/content-fragments/tennis-service-center/assets/pdfs/Net-Generation-Practice-Plan-Red.pdf); [Net Gen Yellow manual](https://www.usta.com/content/dam/usta/content-fragments/curriculum-center/coach-samples/assets/Net-Generation-Coaches-Community-Yellow-Ball-Manual-Sample.pdf)). The plans tell coaches to run each practice plan twice, which makes about 16 sessions per level.

Tennis Canada pushes play even earlier. Every lesson starts and ends with an "I Can Play Points" activity. Play activities run 15 to 20 minutes and practice activities 8 to 12, and no practice activity should run **more than 2 minutes beyond the group's average age** ([Tennis Canada](https://assets.tenniscanada.com/production/PDFs/LearnToPlay_ENG.pdf)).

Adults follow the same play-early logic. USTA Tennis 101 runs six classes of about 55 to 60 minutes. It moves from foam or red balls to orange and then green, and finishes with a 45-minute block of team match play ([USTA Tennis 101](https://www.usta.com/content/dam/usta/content-fragments/tennis-service-center/assets/pdfs/USTA-Tennis-101-for-Adults.pdf)). ITF Tennis Xpress has beginners serving, rallying and scoring from the first of six 90-minute sessions ([Munster Tennis](https://www.munstertennis.ie/tennis-xpress.html)).

Federations publish plans for groups, not private lessons. Translated to a private lesson, the template is:
- a play game;
- two or three practice blocks, none longer than the student's age plus two minutes;
- a closing game;
- a take-home challenge.

Keep coach talk under about a minute per block. Most federation games are written for groups, so in a one-to-one lesson the coach becomes the partner, feeder or catcher, and "beat your record" scoring replaces team scoring.

For semi-private and small-group sessions, the LTA's maximum ratios set the ceiling:
- 1:10 for under-10s;
- 1:12 for older juniors and adults, with 2 to 6 players per court as the optimum;
- 1:4 for tots without parental help ([LTA ratios](https://www.lta.org.uk/4abcbb/siteassets/about-lta/file/coach-player-ratios.pdf)).

Adults need a different tone. ITF adult-learning guidance, seen only as a search excerpt, says adults "need to hit lots of balls", learn actively, and should get individual feedback while they keep playing. It adds that players returning after years away may be apprehensive ([ITF CSSR](https://itfcoachingreview.com/index.php/journal/article/view/453)). For older players, the LTA's Walking Tennis guide asks coaches to keep tips "light and easy to follow", limit technique "to fundamentals" and leave plenty of time for matches ([LTA Walking Tennis](https://www.lta.org.uk/4ac92d/siteassets/play/adult/file/coach-guide.pdf)).

### Moving up: use the top competency sheet as the exit test

The USTA moves a child from orange to green when the child turns 9, earns 1,000 orange play points, or, for 7- and 8-year-olds only, scores **85% on a coach assessment**. Green to yellow happens at age 11 or at 1,500 green PlayTracker points ([USTA Midwest](https://www.usta.com/en/home/play/youth-tennis/programs/midwest/youth-progression.html)).

Net Generation's assessment sheets score each item from 1 to 3, and the top sheet for each colour works as an exit test ([Net Gen assessments, Red](https://www.usta.com/content/dam/usta/content-fragments/tennis-service-center/assets/pdfs/Net-Generation-Assessments-Pre-Rally-Red.pdf); [Orange](https://www.usta.com/content/dam/usta/content-fragments/tennis-service-center/assets/pdfs/Net-Generation-Assessments-Orange.pdf); [Green](https://www.usta.com/content/dam/usta/content-fragments/tennis-service-center/assets/pdfs/Net-Generation-Assessments-Green.pdf)):
- **Red 1:** rallies crosscourt and down the line from the 36-ft baseline; coordinates a knee bend with the top of the toss; plays best-of-three tiebreaks.
- **Orange 1:** serves to three spots; attacks a weak second serve on the rise; identifies an opponent's game style.
- **Green 1:** combines height, spin and speed to keep an opponent deep; returns on the rise; analyses their own matches.

A workable promotion rule is to move a student up at an average score of about 2.5 out of 3, which approximates the USTA's 85% threshold. This is an inference, not a USTA rule. After the move, expect a dip. The USTA green plan warns that "most players' overall skill level will drop" on the 78-ft court and tells coaches to work on court coverage first ([Net Gen Green](https://www.usta.com/content/dam/usta/content-fragments/curriculum-center/coach-samples/assets/Net-Generation-Coaches-Curriculum-Green-Sample.pdf)).

### Load limits: hours below age, kick after 13, walking pace for seniors

No federation publishes a junior serve "pitch count". The defensible controls come from AMSSM:
- play fewer hours per week than the player's age;
- play fewer tournaments per year than the player's age;
- play multiple sports before adolescence;
- **do not practise kick serves until at least age 13** ([AMSSM](https://www.sportsmedtoday.com/tennis-ct-73.htm)).

Emory's tennis-medicine programme classes junior volume as follows:

| Junior volume | Risk |
|---|---|
| Over 10–16 hrs/week | High |
| 6–10 hrs/week | Intermediate |
| Over 40 matches or over 12 tournaments a year | High |

Emory also flags older male juniors and periods of rapid growth ([Emory](https://www.emoryhealthcare.org/centers-programs/sports-medicine-center/tennis-medicine-program/risk-factors)). Kick serves produce the highest lumbar loading, and elite adolescent servers experience lumbar loads of up to about **four times body weight** ([PMC7801574](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7801574/)). During the adolescent growth spurt, bone growth can outpace how fast muscles and tendons adapt ([PMC12101259](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12101259/)).

Under-tens need movement games, not conditioning tests. None of the Net Generation red or orange plans use timed sprints or spider runs, and agility ladders first appear in the green plan ([Net Gen Green](https://www.usta.com/content/dam/usta/content-fragments/tennis-service-center/assets/pdfs/Net-Generation-Practice-Plan-Green.pdf)). Supervised plyometrics are safe for children aged 5 to 14. The best results came from twice-weekly programmes of 8 to 10 weeks that started at 50 to 60 jumps per session ([Johnson et al. 2011](https://digitalcommons.usu.edu/sped_facpub/639)). The popular "windows of trainability" lack longitudinal evidence ([Ford et al. 2011](https://pure.cardiffmet.ac.uk/en/publications/the-long-term-athlete-development-model-physiological-evidence-an/)), so treat them as a reason to keep training varied, not as a schedule.

Adults carry different risks. Emory rates **10 or more hours a week and age over 40 as high risk**, and lists the elbow, low back, rotator cuff and knee as the common injury sites ([Emory](https://www.emoryhealthcare.org/centers-programs/sports-medicine-center/tennis-medicine-program/risk-factors)). AMSSM advises caution above 6 hours a week ([AMSSM](https://www.sportsmedtoday.com/tennis-ct-73.htm)).

Seniors may gain the most from the sport. In the Copenhagen City Heart Study, tennis players lived **9.7 years longer** than sedentary people, the largest gain among the sports studied, although the study is observational ([PubMed 30193744](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30193744/)). The LTA designed Walking Tennis to address falls prevention, osteoporosis, diabetes, cognitive decline and social isolation. Its rules ban running and jumping and allow a double bounce and a smaller court. It also asks coaches to check in on players' health on arrival and to run a cool-down, which it calls "really important" ([LTA Walking Tennis](https://www.lta.org.uk/4ac92d/siteassets/play/adult/file/coach-guide.pdf)). A physiotherapist adds three habits: warm up for at least five minutes, do strength work twice a week, and practise single-leg balance ([Jackson Clinics](https://thejacksonclinics.com/at-your-service-tennis-tips-for-older-players/)).

## Under-tens learn from the game itself, not from explanation

The federations agree on how young children should be taught: change the court, ball and racquet, and teach through games. Tennis Canada rejects "technique first" and uses technique only to answer the question "How can I play better?" ([Tennis Canada](https://assets.tenniscanada.com/production/PDFs/LearnToPlay_ENG.pdf)). The USTA's model for ages 5 to 11 calls for a "cooperative style of coaching", asks coaches to "allow children to make decisions on practice and play", and stresses physical literacy through agility, balance and coordination ([USTA ADM](https://www.usta.com/content/dam/usta/coach-organize/content-fragments/resource-library/assets/pdfs/adm-framework.pdf)). Judy Murray's sequence is individual skill first, then movement, then a partner, with cooperation always before competition ([Judy Murray resource](https://education.gov.scot/media/tunlek1v/jm-tennis-resource-primary.pdf)).

Engagement comes from five devices:
- **Story roles:** Pizza Delivery, Lobster Trap, Fire Ants, Survivor's island.
- **Visible scoring props:** clothespins, playing cards, ball pyramids.
- **"Team best" records instead of winners.** At Red 3, Net Gen advises coaches not to announce team scores and to praise effort.
- **A huddle chant to close the session** ([Net Gen Red community manual](https://www.usta.com/content/dam/usta/content-fragments/tennis-service-center/assets/pdfs/Net-Generation-Community-Manual-Red.pdf)).
- **Characters and award ladders:** the LTA's "Tennisables" characters and task-card awards give children frequent, visible progress ([LTA Tennis at Home](https://www.lta.org.uk/play-compete/lta-youth/tennis-at-home/)).

No federation curriculum that the research could open is written for 3- to 5-year-olds. Net Generation starts at 5 and Tennis Canada's red stage at 6. Running the games below with tots is therefore an adaptation, supported by LTA club tots programmes: 30-minute sessions with balloons, beach balls, beanbags and 17 to 19 inch racquets ([Kinetic Tennis Tots](https://clubspark.lta.org.uk/KineticTennisCoaching/Coaching/Junior/TotsTennis)).

Before a racquet, use Judy Murray's ladder. It runs from balloons, which move slowly enough that everyone succeeds, to chiffon squares and fluff balls, then beanbags that do not roll away, then a ball on the floor, then a bouncing ball. The hand always comes before the racquet ([Judy Murray resource](https://education.gov.scot/media/tunlek1v/jm-tennis-resource-primary.pdf)). A practical tots-to-red sequence follows from the sources:

1. Balloon, hit with the hand.
2. Balloon, hit with a paper-plate racquet.
3. Beanbag balanced on the strings.
4. Big ball rolled along the ground.
5. Red ball rolled with the racquet edge.
6. Bounce and catch.
7. Taps on the strings.
8. Drop-hit to a target.
9. Fed ball.

The research found a claim that balloons should not be used under age 4, but could not verify it. Treat balloons as a supervised prop and check local safety guidance before using them with three-year-olds.

### Kids' faults are fixed by choosing the constraint

The federations do not publish a list of faults for 4- to 8-year-olds. The list below is inferred from the teaching points that recur across their materials, and each fault already has a game built to correct it.

|---|---|---|---|

The sources disagree on grips for 5- and 6-year-olds:
- Tennis Canada uses a continental grip for floor rallies and volleys.

## Groundstrokes: zones build depth, lanes build direction, feeds give way to points

Every curriculum moves groundstroke work from closed drills to open ones. Tennis Australia's squad manual states it as a rule: "Drills/Activities move from closed to open". It adds that 80% of shots require the player to move 3 m or less, that most activities should be scored first to 3, 5 or 7, and that the "worst mistake is hitting into the net" ([Tennis Australia Tennis Time manual](https://www.tennis.com.au/wp-content/uploads/2010/08/Drills-and-Activities.pdf)). The USTA green X Drill shows the same ladder inside a single drill: hand-feed, then racquet-feed from across the net, then faster coach feeds, then competitive play ([Net Gen Green](https://www.usta.com/content/dam/usta/content-fragments/tennis-service-center/assets/pdfs/Net-Generation-Practice-Plan-Green.pdf)).

Two gates from the sources tell you when to open a drill up:
- about **70% success on the fed pattern** ([Sportplan slice plan, 2026](https://www.sportplan.net/drills/Tennis/trends/slice-backhand-tactical-weapon-2025-04.jsp));
- a **10-ball rally** at the current distance ([CoachTube Ten to Move On](https://coachtube.com/course_lesson/best-tennis-drills-for-coaches/ten-to-move-on-groundstroke-consistency-accuracy/19914768)).

In practice, junior and adult players get different versions of the same work (this split follows the USTA's own adult plan, but the allocation is a coaching synthesis):
- **Juniors:** competitive rotation games with high, deep feeds.
- **Adult beginners:** the USTA step-back rally with slower balls.
- **NTRP 3.5–4.5:** coach-fed match patterns first, then scored crosscourt points.

Grip standards differ by level:
- **Adult beginners:** the USTA teaches the Eastern "shake hands" forehand with grip pressure of about 3 out of 10 and a low-to-high "6 o'clock to 12 o'clock" brush ([USTA forehand basics](https://www.usta.com/en/home/improve/tips-and-instruction/national/learning-the-basics--forehand.html)).
- **Juniors:** semi-western is the grip most commonly taught today, with Eastern as a stepping stone ([PlayYourCourt](https://www.playyourcourt.com/news/eastern-vs-western-grip-tennis/?amp=1)).
- **Two-handed backhand:** the non-dominant top hand is the "driver", and the finish has that elbow pointing across the net ([USTA, Davies](https://www.usta.com/en/home/improve/tips-and-instruction/national/improve-your-tennis-game--the-driving-force-in-your-two-handed-b.html)).
- **Slice:** continental grip, a high-to-low path of about 45 degrees and a face opened 10 to 15 degrees ([Sportplan](https://www.sportplan.net/drills/Tennis/trends/slice-backhand-tactical-weapon-2025-04.jsp)).

USTA green plans introduce continental-grip slice and one-handed chips to 9- to 11-year-olds alongside topspin. Lee Tennis Co should therefore not hold the slice back until the teen years.

Most of these faults are one chain, not separate problems. A late or missing unit turn produces late contact and poor spacing, and the player then compensates with the arm or wrist. The practical rule: **check whether the turn is finished by the bounce before correcting anything about the arm.**

## The serve starts as a throw and must be managed as a load

Every source builds the serve the same way: an overarm throw, then hitting down from above the head, then serving from close in, then moving back. Tennis Ireland's red-ball lesson starts with throwing at upside-down cones and moves to a "hand to racket" push-down from above the head ([Tennis Ireland](https://www.tennisireland.ie/wp-content/uploads/2023/05/Primary-School-Over-8s.pdf)). USTA plans add a three-level throw (into the box, past the service line, past the baseline), a quarterback spiral throw that spells out the trophy position, and an orange-ball serve that starts on the service line ([Net Gen Green](https://preview.usta.com/content/dam/usta/content-fragments/tennis-service-center/assets/pdfs/Net-Generation-Practice-Plan-Green.pdf); [Net Gen Yellow](https://preview.usta.com/content/dam/usta/content-fragments/tennis-service-center/assets/pdfs/Net-Generation-Practice-Plan-Yellow.pdf); [Net Gen Orange sample](https://preview.usta.com/content/dam/usta/content-fragments/curriculum-center/coach-samples/assets/Net-Generation-Coaches-Curriculum-Orange-Sample.pdf)). Feel Tennis's adult-beginner lesson follows the same order: a two-ball toss and throw, continental grip, pronation, fluid swings, then "serve in two parts" ([Feel Tennis](https://www.feeltennis.net/serve-lesson-for-beginners/)).

The technical order is grip and edge first, then pronation, then rhythm, then spin and targets. Feel Tennis insists the grip be changed to continental "before doing any corrective exercises" and warns that the grip takes **3 to 6 months** to feel comfortable ([Feel Tennis, waiter's tray](https://www.feeltennis.net/correcting-the-waiters-serve/); [Feel Tennis beginner lesson](https://www.feeltennis.net/serve-lesson-for-beginners/)). A kneeling serve at the T "forces a player to really extend the racket head up" ([Human Kinetics](https://us.humankinetics.com/blogs/excerpt/thrust-launch-serve-technique)). Emory's guidance sets the age order: **delay the kick serve until after 13, hit flat serves before adding spin, and bend the knees more** to reduce shoulder and elbow torque ([Emory wellness](https://www.emoryhealthcare.org/centers-programs/sports-medicine-center/tennis-medicine-program/wellness)).

**Serve volume has no published age limit.** No federation, including the USTA, LTA, ITF and Tennis Australia, publishes a serve count by age. The best available research anchor is Myers and colleagues. They estimate about **40 serves per set** and up to about **5,400 serves in a season**, and their 21-step return-to-serve programme never raises volume and intensity at the same time ([Myers et al. 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5089352)). Emory adds three volume limits ([Emory wellness](https://www.emoryhealthcare.org/centers-programs/sports-medicine-center/tennis-medicine-program/wellness)):
- fewer than 40 USTA singles matches a year for juniors;
- training hours no higher than the player's age;
- a 10-minute dynamic warm-up.

For Lee Tennis Co this suggests three practical rules:
- Red to green players get short serve blocks mixed into play rather than baskets of serves.
- Weekly serve volume should stay steady, never rising in count and intensity (kick or maximum pace) in the same week.
- Older players can use a shorter take-back without extra shoulder cost. A University of Kentucky study found no significant difference in shoulder muscle activation between the abbreviated and traditional serve ([UKY](https://scholars.uky.edu/en/publications/a-comparison-of-muscle-activations-during-traditional-and-abbrevi/)).

### Return and overhead

The USTA says green-ball players "need at least two types of returns": a rally return from behind the baseline and an attacking return taken on the rise ([Net Gen Green](https://preview.usta.com/content/dam/usta/content-fragments/tennis-service-center/assets/pdfs/Net-Generation-Practice-Plan-Green.pdf)). Its return games teach this through constraints rather than explanation. The server gets one serve, the server stands on the service line, or the returner stands inside the baseline.

Timing evidence supports the cue "split as they hit". In 12 expert players, the split-step landing came about **133 ms after the server's contact** and lateral movement began about 177 ms after it ([Navia et al. 2022](https://researchportal.port.ac.uk/en/publications/the-spatiotemporal-control-of-expert-tennis-players-when-returnin/)).

The overhead follows its own ladder:
1. Cooperative drop-hit lobs.
2. Coach-fed lobs with players shadowing.
3. Smash-and-touch-the-net repeats.
4. Movement-back throwing.
5. Lob games.

Teaching the scissor kick relies almost entirely on video. No federation documents a bounce-overhead drill, so letting very high lobs bounce first is the obvious easier version.

## Net play and footwork climb from catch-and-tap to live two-on-one

Volley teaching follows four steps across the youth curricula:
1. **Catch, then tap, at the net.** The LTA's Volley Catch has partners stand about 1 m from the net; the receiver taps a shoulder-height throw back, first with the hand, then with a racquet ([LTA](https://www.lta.org.uk/siteassets/education/file/secondary-pe-inc-tl-red-and-cardio/body--ball---volley-catch.pdf)).
2. **Cooperative volley patterns**, such as figure-8 and V volleys.
3. **Approach, split, volley sequences.**
4. **Live two-on-one and return-and-come-in games.**

Pro-level drills sit on top of that ladder:
- the **Romanian Davis Cup (RDC) drill**, which Wayne Bryan says builds "independence of the hands and feet" ([TennisResources](https://www.tennisresources.com/?p=9389));
- Wayne Bryan's **Four Corner** volley drill, which chases 50 volleys in a row from behind the service line ([TennisResources](https://www.tennisresources.com/?p=9435)).

For both pro drills, video is the better teacher than the thin written setups.

Approach rules are simple: approach off short balls, not well-struck deep ones. Closing too far invites the lob, and closing too little invites the pass ([TennisCompanion](https://tenniscompanion.org/approach-shot/)). Value depth over power, turn early, move laterally rather than sprinting straight at the ball, and do not jump into the shot ([MyTennisHQ](https://mytennishq.com/tennis-approach-shots-5-drills-to-improve-them/)).

Footwork has the best laboratory evidence in the whole topic:
- **The split step speeds response.** In 10 collegiate players, a split step cut movement time to **764 ms from 868 ms**, but only when the landing coincided with the directional cue ([Uzu et al. 2009](https://lida.sport-iat.de/twm/Record/4017156?lng=en)).
- **There is no single correct timing.** Split timing varies by individual and by situation ([Filipcic et al. 2017](https://sponet.de/sponet/Record/4042098)). That argues against drilling one fixed rhythm. The better cue is "hop as they swing, land as you read".

USTA plans teach these movement patterns:
- crossover-then-shuffle recovery, square stance first and then open;
- a hop move on topspin approaches and a carioca on slice approaches;
- outside foot first on advanced volleys;
- drop-step and crossover, never backpedalling, for overheads ([Net Gen Orange](https://www.usta.com/content/dam/usta/content-fragments/tennis-service-center/assets/pdfs/Net-Generation-Practice-Plan-Orange.pdf); [Net Gen Green](https://www.usta.com/content/dam/usta/content-fragments/tennis-service-center/assets/pdfs/Net-Generation-Practice-Plan-Green.pdf)).

Mark Kovacs calls recovery movement "arguably the most important type of training for tennis-specific movement" (quoted from a search excerpt) ([Kovacs Academy](https://kovacsacademy.com/2020/09/24/tennis-movement-footwork-using-common-language-a-recovery-movement-focus)).

### Four net "rules" are beginner defaults, not laws

Popular coaching channels contest four net "rules" that federation materials state as defaults:
- **"Always continental" on volleys.** 2MinuteTennis argues against a strict continental volley grip ([video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=WOZJ1dNFmfU)).
- **"Racquet head always above the wrist."** 2MinuteTennis also argues against this ([video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=3z683nkuBqo)).
- **"Never stand in no-man's-land."** Daily Tennis Lesson lists situations where mid-court is correct ([video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ENSLigmlqMY)).
- **"Always split before a volley."** PlayYourCourt and Essential Tennis both question this ([PlayYourCourt video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=i-x_NmAJ_oA); [Essential Tennis video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=LQvsolevkJU)).

Only the video titles were checked, not their content. Teach the LTA and Net Gen defaults to beginners and juniors, and give adult intermediate and advanced players the nuance.

## Scoring rules teach tactics, doubles and nerves

The standard live-ball games are built from three levers:
- **How the point starts:** a serve, a drop-hit, or "two hits to start".
- **What earns a point:** a bonus for changing direction, plus-or-minus scoring, or points for forcing the opponent wide.
- **Where the ball may go:** half court, crosscourt only, or a deep zone.

A coach can turn any game situation into a game by setting these three levers ([Wikipedia: Tennis games](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tennis_games); [Net Gen Green](https://www.usta.com/content/dam/usta/content-fragments/tennis-service-center/assets/pdfs/Net-Generation-Practice-Plan-Green.pdf)). The commonly taught five situations are serving, returning, both players at the baseline, approaching or at the net, and passing or defending. Treat that list as coaching convention, not a standard. Tennis Australia uses four areas: serving, returning, baseline and net ([Tennis Australia strategy, search excerpt](https://tennis.com.au/learn/how-to-play/strategy)).

Crosscourt is the default pattern for a geometric reason. The net is 3 ft high in the middle against 3.5 ft at the posts, and the crosscourt diagonal is **82.5 ft in singles and 86 ft in doubles, against 78 ft down the line** ([TalkingTennis](https://talkingtennis.net/blog-posts/smart-tennis-why-you-should-hit-a-lot-more-balls-cross-court)). The resulting rule is "aggressive patience" crosscourt, changing direction only on a short ball. The USTA builds green and yellow players' tactics on "five ball controls": direction, height, depth, speed and spin ([Net Gen Green](https://www.usta.com/content/dam/usta/content-fragments/tennis-service-center/assets/pdfs/Net-Generation-Practice-Plan-Green.pdf)).

### Doubles progression

The USTA doubles progression runs in four steps:
1. **One-up/one-back positioning,** with players rotating through all four roles ([Net Gen Yellow](https://www.usta.com/content/dam/usta/content-fragments/tennis-service-center/assets/pdfs/Net-Generation-Practice-Plan-Yellow.pdf)).
2. **Diagonal poaching off the serve.** The poacher "moves diagonally forward as serve bounces", and the coaching mindset is to "poach as if they are alone" on that side ([Net Gen Yellow](https://www.usta.com/content/dam/usta/content-fragments/tennis-service-center/assets/pdfs/Net-Generation-Practice-Plan-Yellow.pdf)).
3. **Two up,** through Gladiator Dubz and Both Sides Attack.
4. **I-formation and Australian formation** for league and high-school players.

USHSTA adds the poaching detail: move on an angle and finish "two steps past the center service line", volleying at the opponent's feet or into a sharp angle ([USHSTA](https://ushsta.org/?p=542)).

In the I-formation:
- The net player crouches on the centre service line about 10 ft from the net and gives hand signals.
- The server ideally serves down the T and moves to the side opposite the net player.
- Mixing the I with Australian formation keeps returners guessing ([Wikipedia: I-formation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/I-formation_(tennis)); [TennisServer](https://www.tennisserver.com/wildcards/wildcards_02_10.html)).

For seniors, a columnist's tips favour short points: start inside the baseline, use the lob "to break the rhythm or catch your breath", and keep the ball low with slice ([Walters, SW News Media](https://swnewsmedia.com/lakeshore_weekly/news/opinion/columnists/commentary-tennis-tips-for-senior-players/article_1c2a8197-2623-5dd4-b459-6714ccdf3db9.html)).

### Mental skills

Mental skills follow the same logic: make the routine compulsory and add pressure through scoring.

Loehr's four-stage between-point routine is:
1. Positive physical response: shoulders back, walk with purpose.
2. Relaxation: look at the strings so the eyes have somewhere to rest.
3. Preparation: decide the first shot while looking across the net.
4. The serve or return ritual ([Tennis.com](https://www.tennis.com/news/articles/playing-with-fire-the-delicate-role-of-emotion-in-pro-and-rec-tennis)).

The "16 seconds" is "a starting place", not a formula, and each player should build their own version ([TennisOne excerpt](https://tennisone.tennisplayer.net/content/MentalToughness/Loehr/training2/train2.htm)).

USTA Player Development packages breathing, self-talk, imagery and goal-setting into routines and asks coaches to create stress in practice. Its cue words include "in control", "trust it" and "go for it" ([USTA PD, search excerpt](https://www.playerdevelopment.usta.com/news/habits_for_tennis_success_package_mental_skills_into_routines)). The ITF frames the same skills as the "5 Cs": commitment, concentration, control, confidence and communication ([ITF CSSR, search excerpt](https://www.itfcoachingreview.com/index.php/journal/article/view/64)).

The LTA's pressure ideas need no equipment:
- second serves only;
- start every service game 0-30 down;
- two points for winning at the net;
- a rally of four before a point counts ([LTA](https://www.lta.org.uk/play/tennis-tips-and-techniques/how-to-get-more-from-your-tennis-practices/)).

For green-ball children, Net Gen's character themes (for example "Resilience: Bounce back") point to simpler mental work: one cue word and one breath, not a full Loehr routine.

### Group formats

Cardio Tennis is the group format worth knowing for semi-private and small-group sessions. It uses low-compression balls, a target of **65 to 85% of maximum heart rate**, sideline stations so no one stands still, and music. The optimum is 10 to 12 players per court, with a minimum of 5 ([LTA Cardio Tennis](https://lta.org.uk/4912a5/siteassets/roles/teachers/file/cardio-tennis/cardio-tennis-introduction.pdf)).

In one-to-one lessons, the rotation games (King of the Court, Champs and Chumps) give way to games that work with two players: Plus/Minus, Crosscourt Points, Serve It Out and Moonball Challenge.

## Conclusion

The research supports one main idea: **in tennis coaching, the constraint is the correction**. Across federations, the fix for a fault is almost never a longer explanation. It is a changed task: a cone to catch in, a zone the ball must reach, a lane to stay inside, a scoring rule that punishes the habit. Because the same ~30 games scale from red ball to seniors by changing only the ball, the court and how the point starts, a private coach does not need a separate curriculum for every age. What Lee Tennis Co needs is a placement check (the Red 1, Orange 1 and Green 1 competency sheets for juniors; the NTRP gap for adults), a shared game library, and the discipline to ask "is the preparation finished by the bounce?" before touching the arm. Most groundstroke, serve and net faults chain back to preparation timing and grip, so diagnosing upstream saves weeks.

The evidence is thinnest where the stakes are highest, so a few habits would put Lee Tennis Co ahead of the published guidance:
- **Serve load:** no federation sets a serve count by age. Logging serve volume per student and never raising volume and intensity in the same week is a practical safeguard the literature implies but no one mandates.
- **Age bands:** these conflict by up to two or three years across federations, so readiness should be scored and recorded, which also gives parents a visible progress report.
- **Dated curricula:** the core USTA curricula date from 2017 to 2019.
- **Unwatched videos:** none of the videos were watched.

A quick review pass, previewing each video assigned and checking the USTA coach portal for newer plans, would close most of the remaining uncertainty.
